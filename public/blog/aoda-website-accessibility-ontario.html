AODA Website Accessibility: A Practical Guide for Ontario Businesses

By ARKA · Published November 24, 2026

The short answer: under the Accessibility for Ontarians with Disabilities Act (AODA), designated public sector organizations and private or non-profit organizations with 50 or more employees in Ontario must make their public websites and web content conform to WCAG 2.0 Level AA. The requirement took full effect on January 1, 2021 and covers web content posted after January 1, 2012. Two success criteria are excepted: live captions and pre-recorded audio descriptions.

This guide is general information, not legal advice. For compliance decisions, rely on the Government of Ontario's official guidance, How to make websites accessible, and the Integrated Accessibility Standards Regulation (O. Reg. 191/11).

Who the website requirement applies to

OrganizationWebsite requirement
Designated public sector organizationsPublic websites and web content must meet WCAG 2.0 Level AA
Private and non-profit, 50+ employees in OntarioPublic websites and web content must meet WCAG 2.0 Level AA
Private and non-profit, fewer than 50 employeesNot required for websites under this rule, though other AODA standards (such as customer service) still apply

The earlier milestone, from January 1, 2014, required new or significantly refreshed websites from covered organizations to meet WCAG 2.0 Level A. Level AA includes everything in Level A, so meeting AA today covers both.

What counts as "web content"

The requirement applies to public-facing websites and the content on them: pages, forms, documents you publish online, images and media. Internal sites such as intranets are not covered by this requirement. Ontario's guidance also describes situations where it may not be practicable to meet the requirements; read it before relying on any exception.

WCAG 2.0 AA in plain language

WCAG (Web Content Accessibility Guidelines) is organised around four principles. In practice, for a business website, they translate into the following:

Perceivable

  • Meaningful images have text alternatives; decorative images are hidden from screen readers.
  • Pre-recorded video has captions.
  • Text has enough contrast against its background (4.5:1 for normal text at Level AA).
  • Content can be zoomed to 200% without breaking.

Operable

  • Everything works with a keyboard alone, with a visible focus indicator.
  • No keyboard traps in menus, pop-ups or embedded widgets.
  • Moving or auto-playing content can be paused.
  • Pages have descriptive titles, and links make sense out of context.

Understandable

  • The page language is declared.
  • Form fields have visible labels, and errors are explained in text.
  • Navigation is consistent from page to page.

Robust

  • HTML is valid and uses real elements (headings, buttons, lists) rather than styled boxes.
  • Custom components expose their name, role and state to assistive technology.

The most common failures we see

  • Low-contrast text, especially light grey on white or text over photos.
  • Images of text and missing alt text on meaningful images.
  • Unlabelled form fields that rely on placeholder text.
  • Menus and modals that can't be opened, used or closed with a keyboard.
  • Headings used for styling instead of structure, so screen-reader users can't navigate.
  • PDFs published without tags or reading order.
  • Videos without captions.

A practical checklist for your next build or redesign

  1. Write "WCAG 2.0 Level AA" into the project scope and acceptance criteria.
  2. Choose a colour palette that passes contrast checks before design starts.
  3. Use real HTML elements for headings, buttons, links, lists and forms.
  4. Test every page with a keyboard only: Tab, Shift+Tab, Enter, Space and Escape.
  5. Run automated checks (for example Lighthouse or axe), then test manually with a screen reader such as VoiceOver or NVDA; automated tools catch only part of the issues.
  6. Caption video and provide transcripts where useful.
  7. Make published documents accessible, or publish the content as a web page instead.
  8. Re-test after each significant content change, not just at launch.

Why it's worth doing even if you're under 50 employees

Accessible websites are easier for everyone to use: clearer structure, readable text, forms that explain themselves and pages that work on any device. The same structure helps search engines and AI assistants understand your content. And if your business grows past the threshold, an accessible site is one less thing to fix.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does AODA apply to small businesses' websites?

The website requirement applies to designated public sector organizations and to private and non-profit organizations with 50 or more employees in Ontario. Smaller organizations aren't required to meet it for their websites, but other AODA standards still apply to them.

Which WCAG version does AODA require?

WCAG 2.0, Level AA, with exceptions for live captions and pre-recorded audio descriptions.

Do I have to fix old content?

The requirement covers web content posted after January 1, 2012. Check Ontario's guidance for how it applies to older content and documents on your site.

Is an accessibility overlay enough for compliance?

Overlays don't fix underlying issues such as missing labels, poor structure or keyboard traps. Accessibility has to be built into the site's code and content.
