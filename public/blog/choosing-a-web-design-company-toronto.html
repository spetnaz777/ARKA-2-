How to Choose a Web Design Company in Toronto

By ARKA · Published November 24, 2026

The short answer: choose a Toronto web design company by checking four things before price: live sites they have actually built (and that still load fast on a phone), who will do the work day to day, what you will own when the project ends, and how the site will be found and accessed by real people. A good proposal answers all four in writing. Price only makes sense once you can compare like with like.

Toronto and the wider GTA have hundreds of agencies, studios and freelancers offering websites, from template installs to full product teams. That choice is useful, but it makes proposals hard to compare. This guide is the checklist we would give a friend hiring someone other than us: what to ask, what to watch for, and what a fair proposal should include.

What a web design company should actually deliver

"A website" can mean anything from a theme with your logo dropped in to a custom application. Before comparing quotes, agree on what the finished product must do. For most service businesses that means:

  • Clear service pages that explain what you do, for whom and where, each with its own URL, title and description.
  • A fast, mobile-first build. Most local searches happen on a phone, so the mobile version is the real website.
  • Enquiry paths that work: forms that reliably reach the right inbox, click-to-email or call links, and booking if you need it.
  • Search foundations: per-page metadata, structured data, an XML sitemap, clean URLs and HTML that search engines can read without running JavaScript.
  • Accessibility: keyboard support, readable contrast, real headings and labelled form fields.
  • Hand-over: access to the code, hosting, domain and analytics in your name, plus notes on how to update the site.

Ten questions to ask before you hire

1. Can I see live sites you built, and talk about what you did on each?

Screenshots are easy to borrow. Ask for live URLs, open them on your phone, and ask which parts the company actually did: design, development, copy, SEO or all of it. A good answer is specific.

2. Who will design and build my site?

Many firms sell with a senior person and deliver with someone else, sometimes offshore or subcontracted. That isn't automatically bad, but you should know who you will be working with and whether you can talk to them directly.

3. Is the design custom or based on a template?

Templates can be fine for a simple, low-stakes site. If the site is meant to win clients, ask how the layout will be designed around your services and customers rather than around a theme's demo content.

4. What will I own at the end?

You should own the domain, the content, the design files and either the code or a clear licence to it. Hosting and analytics accounts should be in your name or transferable. Ask directly what happens if you stop working together.

5. How will the site be found in search?

Ask what is included: unique titles and descriptions for every page, structured data, a sitemap submitted to Google Search Console, redirects from any old URLs and prerendered or server-rendered HTML. "SEO-friendly" without specifics is not an answer.

6. How do you handle accessibility?

In Ontario, the AODA requires private and non-profit organizations with 50 or more employees to make public websites meet WCAG 2.0 Level AA. Even if your business is smaller, building to that standard is good practice. Ask how they test it. Our AODA website accessibility guide explains what is involved.

7. How will enquiries reach me?

Forms that silently fail are one of the most common and expensive website problems. Ask where submissions go, whether they are logged, how spam is filtered and whether they can flow into your CRM.

8. How fast will the site load on a phone?

Ask how they measure performance (Google's Lighthouse and Core Web Vitals are the usual tools) and what they do about large images, video and third-party scripts.

9. What does the process look like, and what do you need from me?

Good firms are clear about stages, review points and what you must provide: content, photos, logins and decisions. Delays on the client side are the most common reason projects run long.

10. What happens after launch?

Ask about fixes after launch, updates, hosting costs, and whether ongoing support is optional or bundled into a long contract.

Red flags

  • Guaranteed rankings. Nobody can guarantee a position in Google.
  • No live examples, or examples that are clearly templates with different logos.
  • Domain or hosting registered in the agency's name with no written plan to transfer it.
  • Long lock-in contracts before you have seen any design work.
  • Vague SEO promises with no list of what will actually be done.
  • Reviews or testimonials you can't trace to a real business.

Toronto and GTA specifics worth raising

  • Service areas. If you serve Mississauga, Vaughan or Markham from a Toronto base, the site should say so clearly. Location pages help only when each one carries genuinely useful information; near-identical city pages rarely do.
  • Privacy. Contact forms collect personal information. Ontario businesses generally fall under PIPEDA, the federal private-sector privacy law, so your site should have a privacy policy that matches what the forms actually collect.
  • Newsletter sign-ups. If the site collects emails for marketing, Canada's Anti-Spam Legislation (CASL) expects consent, sender identification and a working unsubscribe.
  • Google Business Profile. For local searches, your profile and your website work together. Ask whether the company will make sure the two are consistent.

Agency, freelancer or studio?

Larger agencies bring more people and can run several workstreams at once, at the cost of account-management layers and higher overhead. Freelancers can be excellent and affordable, but capacity and continuity depend on one person. Small, founder-led studios sit between the two: you work directly with the person designing and building, with a process designed for business sites rather than one-off favours. Whichever you choose, the questions above apply equally.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a website cost in Toronto?

It varies widely with scope: number of pages, custom design, integrations, content and SEO work. Compare proposals on a written scope rather than headline price, and be wary of quotes that don't list what is included.

How long does it take to build a business website?

A focused marketing site with content ready can move in weeks or less; larger sites, custom features and web applications take longer. Content and feedback from the client are the most common causes of delay.

Should my web designer handle SEO?

At minimum, the build should include technical SEO foundations: unique titles and descriptions, structured data, a sitemap, redirects from old URLs and crawlable HTML. Ongoing content and link-building are usually separate work.

Do I need AODA compliance for my website?

Private and non-profit organizations in Ontario with 50 or more employees must make public websites meet WCAG 2.0 Level AA. Smaller organizations aren't required to for websites, but building accessibly is good practice. Check Ontario's official guidance for your situation.
